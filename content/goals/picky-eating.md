Being wary of new foods is a normal stage for many young children. It can take 10 or more tastes before a child accepts a new food, so a "no" today is not a "no" forever. The goal is a calm table where your child keeps meeting new foods without pressure.

## Background: Who decides what

A helpful split of jobs: you decide *what* food is offered and *when*; your child decides *whether* to eat it and *how much*. Pressure, punishment or bribes usually backfire and make new foods feel like a battle.

Sources: aap-picky

## Background: When to check in with a doctor

If you are concerned about your child's diet or growth, talk to your pediatrician or health visitor. They can help troubleshoot and check that your child is getting the nutrients they need to grow and develop.

## Win: One family meal with one safe food

### Action
Serve the same meal to everyone instead of cooking a separate dish for your child, and make sure there is at least one food on the table they usually eat, like bread, rice or a fruit they like.

### Say this
"Tonight we have chicken, peas and rice. You can start with whatever you like."

### What to expect
Knowing a familiar food is there lowers the stakes, and your child sees new foods without being forced to eat them. They may fill up on the safe food at first. That's okay. The exposure still counts.

## Win: Tiny portions, many tries

### Action
Offer a new or refused food in a very small amount, like one or two pieces, alongside foods they know. Offer it again on other days, maybe prepared differently (raw versus cooked, grated versus sliced).

### Say this
"Here's one little piece of carrot to meet. You don't have to eat it."

### What to expect
It can take 10 or more tastes before a food is accepted, and touching, smelling or licking it are steps along the way. Small portions mean less waste and less pressure for both of you.

aap-picky, nhs-fussy

## Win: Take the pressure off

### Action
Don't force, bribe or punish around food, and avoid using dessert as a reward. If something isn't eaten, calmly take it away without comment. Praise trying, not finishing.

### Say this
"You don't have to eat it. You can leave it on the side of your plate."

### What to expect
Many parents find this feels backwards at first. With less pressure, mealtimes usually become calmer, and children often become more willing to try things over the following weeks.

aap-picky, nhs-fussy

## Win: Set a steady meal and snack rhythm

### Action
Keep meals and snacks at roughly regular times, and limit grazing in between. The NHS suggests no more than two healthy snacks a day, so your child arrives at meals ready to eat. Avoid meals when your child is overtired.

### Say this
"The kitchen is closed for now. Snack time is after the park."

### What to expect
Children who come to the table a bit hungry are more open to what's on offer. It can take a few days for a new rhythm to settle in.

### Sources
aap-picky, nhs-fussy

## Win: Eat together, screens away

### Action
Sit down and eat with your child when you can, with screens off and toys away. Let them see you enjoying a variety of foods, including the ones you'd like them to try.

### Say this
"Mmm, these peppers are crunchy. What sound does yours make?"

### What to expect
Children learn a lot about food by copying the people around them. This is a slow-burn habit: you probably won't see change at one meal, but over weeks it shapes what feels normal to eat.
