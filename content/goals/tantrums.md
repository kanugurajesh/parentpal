Tantrums are very common in toddlers. They often start around 18 months and usually become less frequent after age 3. A tantrum isn't a verdict on your parenting: it's what happens when a small child's feelings are bigger than their words. The aim isn't zero tantrums. It's shorter, less frequent ones, and a child who slowly learns what to do with big feelings.

## Background: Why tantrums happen

Common triggers are being tired, hungry, frustrated or wanting attention, and moments of change like leaving the park or switching off a show. Some children are simply more intense by temperament, and that is not caused by parenting. Noticing *when* tantrums tend to happen is often the most useful first step, because many triggers can be planned around: a snack before the shop, an earlier nap, a warning before a transition.

Sources: aap-tantrums, nhs-tantrums

## Background: When to talk to a professional

Talk to your pediatrician, GP or health visitor if you are seriously concerned about your child's behavior, if hitting, kicking or biting is frequent, if you are worried about their development, or if you feel you can't cope. Asking for help is a normal part of parenting, not a failure.

Sources: aap-tantrums, nhs-tantrums, cdc-act-early

## Win: Name the feeling before fixing it

### Action
Get down to your child's eye level, keep your voice low, and say out loud the feeling you think they are having, before you try to solve the problem or set a limit.

### Say this
"You really wanted the blue cup. You're feeling so angry. I'm right here."

### What to expect
Naming a feeling won't end every tantrum on the spot. Over a few weeks, many children start to settle a little faster because they feel understood, and they begin to borrow your words ("I'm mad!") instead of only using their bodies. If you guess the wrong feeling, that's fine. Your child will often correct you, and that is communication too.

## Win: Offer two small choices

### Action
Before a moment that usually sparks a battle (getting dressed, leaving the park, brushing teeth), offer two options that you are happy with either way. Keep it to two. Open questions like "What do you want to wear?" can overwhelm.

### Say this
"Do you want the red socks or the striped socks?" or "Shall we hop or tiptoe to the car?"

### What to expect
Small choices give a toddler some control over a day that is mostly decided for them, which often heads off the power struggle. Sometimes they will refuse both. Repeat the choice calmly once, then kindly choose for them: "Looks like you want me to pick. Striped ones today."

## Win: Warn before a transition

### Action
Give a short heads-up a few minutes before something changes, and attach the warning to something your child can understand: a number of turns, a song, or a timer they can see.

### Say this
"Two more slides, then it's time for shoes."

### What to expect
Warnings work best when you follow through the same way every time. The first few times your child may still protest when time is up. Stay calm, do what you said, and expect it to get easier as they learn that your warnings are predictable.

## Win: Stay close with a time-in

### Action
When your child is overwhelmed (upset or anxious rather than doing something unsafe), stay with them instead of sending them away. Sit nearby, offer comfort if they want it, and wait for their body to calm down. You can stay kind without giving in to the demand that started it.

### Say this
"This is really hard. I'm going to stay with you until your body feels calmer."

### What to expect
Early on, a time-in can take a while. With practice, many children calm more quickly because they know you'll stay. Once they are calm, briefly talk about what happened, and keep the original "no" in place so the tantrum doesn't become the way to get it.

## Win: Draw a clear line on hurting

### Action
If your child hits, kicks, bites or throws things at someone, calmly stop it straight away, every time. Accept the feeling, block the behavior, and offer a safe alternative for the energy.

### Say this
"I won't let you hit. You can stomp your feet or squeeze this pillow as hard as you want."

### What to expect
Expect to repeat this many times. Learning a new habit takes young children lots of practice. If aggression is frequent, getting worse, or you're worried about it, bring it up with your pediatrician or health visitor.
