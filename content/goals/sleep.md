Good sleep is built mostly in the hours *before* bed and across the day: predictable routines, a calm room, daylight and movement. Small, consistent changes tend to work better than big overhauls, and most take a week or two of steady repetition before you see a difference.

## Background: How much sleep does my child need?

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends total sleep per 24 hours, including naps: 4–12 months, 12–16 hours; 1–2 years, 11–14 hours; 3–5 years, 10–13 hours; 6–12 years, 9–12 hours. Children vary, so use these ranges as a guide rather than a target to hit exactly.

Sources: aasm-2016, aap-sleep

## Background: Sleep signs worth checking with a doctor

Mention it to your pediatrician if your child snores loudly most nights, seems to stop breathing or gasp during sleep, has persistent trouble falling or staying asleep despite a steady routine, or is very sleepy or unusually irritable during the day.

Sources: aap-sleep

## Win: Keep a short, same-every-night routine

### Action
Pick three or four simple steps and do them in the same order every night, starting at about the same time. Keep it short (around 20–30 minutes) so it is easy to repeat even on busy days.

### Say this
"Bath, pajamas, book, bed. First bath, then pajamas, then our book."

### What to expect
Routines help children's bodies and minds know that sleep is coming. In the first week there may be some pushback while the new order sinks in. Most families notice smoother bedtimes after one to two weeks of sticking with it.

## Win: Screens off an hour before bed

### Action
Switch off TV, tablets and phones at least 60 minutes before bedtime, and keep devices out of the bedroom overnight. Swap in calm activities like books, puzzles or drawing.

### Say this
"The tablet is going to sleep now, so let's choose a book for your wind-down."

### What to expect
The first few evenings may bring protests, especially if screens were part of winding down. Give a warning before switch-off and offer an appealing replacement. Many children settle more easily once the hour before bed is consistently screen-free.

## Win: Keep wake-up time steady, weekends too

### Action
Choose a wake-up time and keep it within roughly the same window every day, including weekends. Line up naps and bedtime around it.

### Say this
"Good morning! It's wake-up time. Let's open the curtains and say hello to the day."

### What to expect
A steady wake-up time anchors the body clock, which tends to make it easier to fall asleep at night. It can feel hard for a few days if your child has been sleeping in, but bedtime often gets easier within about a week.

## Win: Set up the room for sleep

### Action
Dim the lights during the routine, keep the room comfortably cool (the AAP suggests around 68–72°F / 20–22°C), and move toys out of the bed so it is a place for sleeping, not playing.

### Say this
"Let's turn the lights down low so your room knows it's sleepy time."

### What to expect
Environment changes are quick to make and often help a little straight away. They work best combined with a consistent routine rather than on their own.

## Win: Daylight and movement during the day

### Action
Get outside in daylight, ideally in the morning, and build in active play during the day. Avoid very exciting play right before bed.

### Say this
"Let's go find three interesting leaves before lunch!"

### What to expect
Daytime light and activity support the body clock and healthy tiredness at night. It is a gradual effect, so think of it as part of the overall picture rather than an instant fix.
